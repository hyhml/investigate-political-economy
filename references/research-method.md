## Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order, while recognizing that lower-ranked sources may expose facts omitted above:

1. Laws, executive instruments, regulatory filings, contracts, court records, legislative and audit materials.
2. Government agency records, company filings, exchange announcements, financial statements, and signed agreement texts.
3. Direct interviews and high-quality investigative reporting.
4. Specialist research, labor organizations, local reporting, and civil-society investigations.
5. General news summaries, commentary, and promotional materials.

Search in relevant source languages. Record publication date and event date separately. Preserve direct links. Treat company and government claims as evidence of their stated position, not automatic proof of outcomes.

## Evidence ledger

For each consequential claim, record:

- exact proposition;
- source and source type;
- event date and publication date;
- status: proposed, authorized, signed, closed, disbursed, built, operating, delivered, or realized;
- direct quotation or precise paraphrase;
- what it supports;
- what it cannot support;
- conflicts or corroboration;
- confidence and follow-up search.

Do not confuse a loan ceiling with disbursement, an MOU with a contract, prospective resource value with reserves, an offtake intention with delivery, or temporal proximity with causation.

## Interpretation ledger

For each important political judgment, record:

- judgment;
- author: user, AI, or jointly developed;
- material mechanism;
- supporting claims from the evidence ledger;
- counterevidence;
- rival explanation;
- scope conditions;
- falsifier or evidence that would materially weaken it;
- wording and certainty appropriate for publication;
- user decision.

## Causal discipline

Distinguish:

- direct command or contractual coordination;
- policy-induced convergence of profit-seeking actors;
- structural compatibility without direct funding;
- temporal coincidence;
- retrospective narrative imposed by the analyst.

Prefer the strongest mechanism supported by evidence, not the most dramatic label. A political judgment may be bold while remaining explicit about the mechanism and limit.

## Political-economy mechanisms

Investigate concrete distributions of:

- credit and first-loss risk;
- ownership, collateral, voting, veto, audit, and seizure rights;
- pricing and market access;
- offtake, logistics, inventories, processing, and technical certification;
- labor, land, energy, infrastructure, pollution, and closure costs;
- sanctions, tariffs, military procurement, export controls, and diplomatic leverage;
- profits, taxes, royalties, technology, and bargaining power.

Do not reduce resource-producing or allied states to passive objects. Investigate their strategies, internal classes, state firms, bargaining power, and attempts to capture value.

## Saturation

Treat a stage as provisionally saturated only when:

- the main primary instruments have been found or their absence documented;
- core actors and transaction stages are covered;
- major numbers and statuses are checked;
- opposing and local perspectives have been sought;
- source conflicts are recorded;
- additional searches are no longer revealing new actor classes, mechanisms, or thesis-changing evidence.

Report coverage and remaining gaps. Never equate saturation with certainty.
